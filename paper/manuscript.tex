\begin{abstract}
Claims of fuel saving from maritime route ``optimization'' are difficult to compare when the baseline, the network, and the fuel model are not stated \citep{zis2020}.
This paper specifies all three for a 20-port Indo-Pacific set and a polyline network of shipping lanes distributed with the code.
Port-to-port distances are shortest paths in that network.
Mandatory intermediate ports turn the voyage into an open-path traveling-salesman problem on the resulting metric closure.
Fuel is estimated with the Holtrop--Mennen regression \citep{holtrop1982,holtrop1984}, not with a distance rate scaled by an assumed weather factor.
On all \NConnected{} connected ordered pairs, A* with the great-circle heuristic returns the same distance as Dijkstra.
Lane distance exceeds great-circle distance by a median factor of \MedianDetour{} (mean \MeanDetour{}).
The maximum factor, \MaxDetour{}, is Jebel~Ali--Suez Canal and is a missing-corridor artefact, not an operational detour.
Enumeration of waypoint orders with up to seven hubs is exact.
A permutation genetic algorithm recovers that order in every replicate, as does nearest insertion; reversing the geographic order lengthens the voyage by \ReversedGapMin--\ReversedGapMax\%.
A stylized current field changes fuel by between \CurrentPenalty\% and \(+\CurrentBenefitMax\%\) and does not change the optimal order on any of the \NInstances{} instances.
Under an illustrative bunker price of \BunkerPrice~USD/t and a time-charter of \CharterRate~USD/day, the cost-minimizing speed on the Singapore--Tokyo lane is \EconomicSpeed~knots.
No weather-routing saving is reported.
An earlier comparison against a fixed 15\% allowance was not a saving and has been removed.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Minimum-time weather routing goes back at least to the isochrone construction of \citet{james1957}.
Later work replaced hand-drawn isochrones with dynamic programming, graph search, and field-based models \citep{mannarini2016,zis2020}.
Speed selection is a separate decision.
Cubic resistance makes fuel highly sensitive to speed, while the commercial objective usually mixes bunker cost with a time cost \citep{corbett2009,notteboom2009,psaraftis2013}.
\citet{zis2020} note that published savings are hard to compare because baselines are not standardized.

The software this paper accompanies originally mixed those decisions.
A genetic algorithm sequenced ports.
A separate fuel column was described as the effect of four-dimensional weather.
That column was compared with the same voyage multiplied by 1.15, and the ratio was reported as a saving, including a constant 15\% when the forecast was unavailable.
The resistance routine multiplied Holtrop's wave-resistance coefficient by \(10^{-9}\) to offset the use of the block coefficient in place of Holtrop's \(c_7\).
A current ``following'' and ``adverse'' test used the same angular condition in both branches, so the adverse branch was unreachable.
Fouling was applied twice.
None of those quantities is retained as a result.

The contribution is therefore not a new routing principle.
It is a formulation that can be checked, a resistance model that matches the published regression well enough to produce a service-speed fuel rate of the expected order, and a numerical study that separates lane geometry, waypoint order, current, fouling, and speed.
The study is small on purpose.
Every instance is enumerated where enumeration is cheap, and every negative result is reported.

Section~\ref{sec:problem} states the problem.
Section~\ref{sec:methods} describes the network, the search, and the fuel model.
Section~\ref{sec:design} states what is compared with what.
Section~\ref{sec:results} gives the numbers produced by \texttt{experiments/run\_study.py}.
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} records what the study does not establish.

\section{Problem}
\label{sec:problem}

Let \(G=(V,E)\) be an undirected graph whose vertices are sample points on reported shipping lanes together with a finite port set \(P\subset V\).
An edge between consecutive lane samples has length equal to the great-circle distance of its endpoints.
A port is joined to every lane vertex within 500~km.
Given an origin \(o\in P\), a destination \(d\in P\), and a mandatory intermediate set \(H\subset P\setminus\{o,d\}\), a feasible voyage is a sequence
\begin{equation}
\sigma = (o, h_{\pi(1)}, \ldots, h_{\pi(|H|)}, d),
\end{equation}
where \(\pi\) is a permutation of \(H\).
The geometric length of \(\sigma\) is the sum of shortest-path distances in \(G\),
\begin{equation}
D(\sigma) = \sum_{i=0}^{|\sigma|-2} \mathrm{dist}_G(\sigma_i, \sigma_{i+1}).
\end{equation}
If every edge cost used later is a positive multiple of length, and that multiple does not depend on direction, then every optimal permutation is a shortest-path order in the complete graph on \(\{o\}\cup H\cup\{d\}\) with the metric \(\mathrm{dist}_G\).
Speed through water \(V\) and an along-track current break that equivalence, because fuel then depends on the direction of each segment.

Two voyage costs are used.
The geometric cost is \(D(\sigma)\).
The fuel cost holds speed through water fixed and accumulates fuel along the realized track, with speed over ground
\begin{equation}
V_g = \max(V + c_{\parallel},\, 1~\mathrm{knot}),
\label{eq:sog}
\end{equation}
where \(c_{\parallel}\) is the component of the current along the segment heading, positive when following.
Fuel rate is a function of \(V\) only, so time, and therefore fuel, scales as \(V/V_g\) relative to calm water.
A third, commercial cost is defined only for a fixed path, as a function of speed:
\begin{equation}
C(V) = p_b\, m_f(V) + p_t\, T(V),
\label{eq:cost}
\end{equation}
with bunker price \(p_b\), fuel mass \(m_f\), charter rate \(p_t\), and voyage duration \(T\).
Equation~\eqref{eq:cost} is the standard bunker-plus-time form reviewed by \citet{psaraftis2013}.
The prices used below are scenario values, not a fixture or a bunker quotation.

Emission mass is fuel mass times 3.114~t~CO\(_2\) per tonne of residual fuel, the inventory factor used for residual fuel in the IMO greenhouse-gas studies \citep{imo2020}.
It is not an engine measurement.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Lane network}
The network is the file \texttt{Shipping\_Lanes\_v1.geojson} in the repository: a GeoJSON \texttt{FeatureCollection} of \texttt{MultiLineString} features.
The file has no source, date, or license metadata.
It is treated as a routing graph supplied with the code, not as an AIS product and not as a representation of navigable water.
Vertices are coordinate samples, quantized at \(10^{-6}\) degrees so that shared endpoints merge.
The resulting graph has \NSeaNodes{} sea vertices, \NEdges{} edges including port connectors, and \NComponents{} connected components.
Mean degree of the sea vertices is \MeanDegree.
A graph with mean degree near 2 is a collection of polylines with a modest number of branches, not a mesh of alternative sea routes.
Of the sea vertices, \SeaInPortComponent{} lie in the component that contains the port set.
The other components are lane fragments that this port set never reaches.
All \NPairs{} ordered pairs among the \NPorts{} ports are connected through that component (\NDisconnected{} disconnected pairs).

Great-circle distance uses a spherical Earth of radius 6371~km.
The same formula is the A* heuristic and the edge weight, so the heuristic is admissible and consistent on this graph \citep{hart1968}.
A* distances must therefore match Dijkstra distances \citep{dijkstra1959} up to numerical tolerance.
That identity is checked rather than assumed.

\subsection{Waypoint order}
For \(|H|\le 7\), every permutation is evaluated.
That is the reference optimum.
Two heuristics are compared with it.
Nearest insertion builds an open path by inserting, at each step, the hub and slot with the smallest total length.
The genetic algorithm uses a population of \texttt{24} permutations, \texttt{40} generations, order crossover \citep{davis1985}, swap mutation with probability 0.3, and elitism of two individuals.
It is run from \texttt{20} seeds.
An earlier crossover concatenated both parents and then appended any missing hub, so the child was not a permutation.
That operator is not used.

The geographic order stored in the study file is also evaluated, and so is that order reversed.
The reversed order is a naive baseline.
It is not a competitive heuristic.

\subsection{Resistance and fuel}
Calm-water resistance follows Holtrop and Mennen \citep{holtrop1982,holtrop1984}.
The frictional coefficient is the ITTC-1957 line \citep{ittc1957}.
The form factor is the 1984 expression in the length of run, \(c_{12}\), and \(c_{13}\).
Wave resistance uses \(c_7\), not the block coefficient, in
\begin{equation}
c_1 = 2223105\, c_7^{3.78613}\,(T/B)^{1.07961}\,(90-i_E)^{-1.37565},
\end{equation}
with the piecewise definition of \(c_7\) from \citet{holtrop1984}: \(0.229577(B/L)^{1/3}\) if \(B/L<0.11\), \(B/L\) if \(0.11\le B/L\le 0.25\), and \(0.5-0.0625\,L/B\) otherwise.
For Froude number \(F_n\le 0.4\),
\begin{equation}
R_W = c_1 c_2 c_5 \nabla \rho g \exp\!\left(m_1 F_n^{-0.9} + m_2 \cos(\lambda F_n^{-2})\right).
\end{equation}
Bulb, transom, and correlation terms follow the same sources.
Air drag, \(0.5\rho_a V^2 A_a C_D\) with \(A_a=280~\mathrm{m}^2\) and \(C_D=0.8\), is added outside the hydrostatic regression because it is not part of Holtrop's calm-water residuary.
Brake power uses a quasi-propulsive efficiency of 0.70 and a shaft efficiency of 0.97.
Specific fuel consumption is constant at 170~g/kWh.
There is no engine map, so the model does not represent the rise in specific consumption at low load.

The reported hull is a Panamax container ship with particulars that are internally consistent: \(L=280~\mathrm{m}\), \(B=32.2~\mathrm{m}\), \(T=12.5~\mathrm{m}\), \(C_B=0.65\), \(C_M=0.98\), so \(\nabla=C_B L B T=\Displacement~\mathrm{m}^3\) and \(C_P=C_B/C_M\).
A beam of 40~m, previously labelled Panamax, is not used.
The wetted surface is the Holtrop--Mennen regression, \WettedSurface~m\(^2\) at the reported coefficients, not a round number inserted by hand.
The regression envelope used as a validity flag is \(0.10\le F_n\le 0.45\) and \(0.55\le C_P\le 0.85\).
All speeds reported below fall inside it.
The prediction is still a regression, not a sea trial.

\subsection{Current}
The current field is a sum of Gaussian cores placed on five named systems (Gulf Stream, Kuroshio, Agulhas, an equatorial set, and the Canary Current) plus two eddies.
Core speeds are of order 0.4--2.5~m/s and decay with a 500~km scale inside a 1000~km cutoff.
December--February uses the winter factor stored with each system; June--August uses the summer factor; other months use 1.
This is a schematic climatology.
It is not OSCAR, not Copernicus Marine, and not a forecast.

Only the along-track component enters \eqref{eq:sog}.
A previous implementation compared the current's compass bearing with 90\(^\circ\) and with 270\(^\circ\) using the same predicate in both branches, and it reported a speed in knots that was still in metres per second.
Neither quantity is used.

\subsection{Fouling}
The fouling module applies a scenario multiplier for slime, barnacles, tubeworms, and algae, with a temperature and salinity factor and a 40\% cap.
It is not the ITTC roughness allowance, and it is not a fit to \citet{schultz2007} or \citet{townsin2003}.
Those papers are cited only to locate the published observation that fouling can change required power by amounts of this order.
The numerical study reports the multiplier at a tropical scenario of 28\(^\circ\)C and 35~psu.
Fouling is applied once, at voyage level, and never inside the resistance routine.
Reported corridor fuel is for a clean hull unless a column says otherwise.

\subsection{What is not in the objective}
Lunar phase and a simplified tidal height are computed by the application and are not terms in \(D\), \(m_f\), or \(C\).
Emission-control areas defined in the application lie in the North Sea and on the North American coasts.
None of the study ports falls in those boxes, so the ECA price is inactive here.
The operational forecast client can call external weather APIs.
The numerical study does not.
A piecewise sea-state multiplier remains in the application code.
It is not a result of this paper, because it was previously differenced against an assumed factor of 1.15.

\section{Experimental design}
\label{sec:design}

The study answers six questions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Are all port pairs connected, and by how much do lane distances exceed great-circle distances?
\item Do A* and Dijkstra agree, and what does the timing protocol actually measure?
\item For the stated hub sets, what is the gap of a reversed order, of nearest insertion, and of the genetic algorithm, relative to enumeration?
\item Does January along-track current change the fuel-minimizing order?
\item What fuel rate does the corrected regression give between 12 and 24~knots?
\item For the Singapore--Tokyo lane, which speed in \(\{12,\ldots,24\}\) minimizes \eqref{eq:cost} at \(p_b=\BunkerPrice\)~USD/t and \(p_t=\CharterRate\)~USD/day?
\end{enumerate}
There is no hold-out ocean, no AIS track used as a ground truth, and no comparison with a commercial weather-routing product.
A comparison of that kind would require a current and wave field the repository does not contain.

Distances are in kilometres, fuel in tonnes, and carbon dioxide in tonnes using the factor in Section~\ref{sec:problem}.
The genetic-algorithm budget is \(24\times 40=960\) evaluations of the length cost per seed, plus the initial population.
Enumeration of seven hubs is \(7!=5040\) evaluations.
Both are cheap because a voyage length is a sum of cached port-to-port distances.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Lane distances}
Figure~\ref{fig:detour} shows the distribution of the ratio of lane distance to great-circle distance over the \NConnected{} ordered pairs.
The median ratio is \MedianDetour, the mean of the ratios is \MeanDetour, and the minimum is \MinDetour.
The ratio of mean lengths is smaller than the mean of ratios, which is expected for a right-skewed set.
\PairsAboveOnePointFive{} pairs exceed 1.5.
Mean lane length is \MeanLaneKm~km against a mean great-circle length of \MeanGcKm~km.

The maximum ratio, \MaxDetour, is Jebel~Ali--Suez Canal in both directions: 2{,}322~km by great circle and 10{,}088~km on the lane network (2{,}321.6~km and 10{,}087.7~km before rounding).
The shortest path in the file does enter the Red Sea.
Before that, it runs east from the Gulf of Oman to about 80\(^\circ\)E and only then west through the Gulf of Aden.
The missing piece is a direct Arabian Sea corridor, not the Red Sea itself.
The ratio is evidence of a hole in the graph, not evidence that a ship should sail four times the great-circle distance.
Busan--Yokohama and Busan--Tokyo are the next class of outliers, at ratios 2.33 and 2.31, which is consistent with a lane that follows the Japanese coast rather than the direct crossing.
Singapore--Yokohama is almost the great circle (ratio \MinDetour).
Any paper that reports a single ``optimization percentage'' against the great circle, on this file, is averaging a data defect with a mild coastal detour.

Recomputing A* for every ordered pair took \SecondsAstar~s.
Building one Dijkstra tree from each of the 20 origins took \SecondsDijkstra~s.
The two algorithms agreed to \(10^{-6}\)~km on all \AstarMatches{} pairs.
A* is not faster in this protocol, and the paper does not claim a search speed-up.
The useful statement is the identity: the heuristic does not sacrifice optimality on this weight function.
For a 20-port closure, one Dijkstra tree per origin is the straightforward construction.

\subsection{Waypoint order}
Table~\ref{tab:sequencing} lists the eight instances.
The geographic order in the study file is already a shortest order: its gap against enumeration is zero in every row.
Nearest insertion also has zero gap.
The genetic algorithm matches the enumerated optimum in all 20 seeds on every instance (maximum gap \GaMaxGap\%, success rate \GaMinSuccess\%).
The reversed order is longer by between \ReversedGapMin\% and \ReversedGapMax\%.

That pattern should not be read as a success of the genetic algorithm.
On this metric, with hubs placed along a natural corridor and \(|H|\le 7\), enumeration is exact and nearest insertion also recovers the optimum.
The genetic algorithm is retained because the application accepts an arbitrary hub list, and because the previous crossover was not a permutation operator.
It is not the reason a reversed schedule is improved.
The result that matters is the size of that reversed-order gap: for these corridors, order is worth tens of percent of distance, and a geographic sort already captures it.

On all \NInstances{} instances the fuel-minimizing order in January, at 18~knots, is the same as the shortest order (\NCurrentOrderChanges{} disagreements).
Direction-dependent current is weak relative to the differences between permutations in this schematic field.
A conclusion that current never matters would be stronger than the experiment.
The conclusion the experiment supports is narrower: this current model does not reorder these hub sets.

\subsection{Resistance, fouling, and current}
Figure~\ref{fig:resistance} separates viscous and wave-making resistance.
At 20~knots the model gives \ResistanceTwenty~kN total resistance, \PowerTwenty~MW brake power, and \FuelTwenty~t/day of fuel.
At 18~knots the fuel rate is \FuelEighteen~t/day.
The form factor is \FormFactor, at the low end of the range usually quoted for merchant hulls.
Wave-making is small at 12~knots and is about a third of the viscous term by 24~knots, which is the qualitative behaviour the regression is meant to produce.
These figures are uncalibrated against a trial.
They replace a model whose wave term had been scaled by \(10^{-9}\) and which printed component forces on every call.

Beyond the cap, days since cleaning are not an additional decision variable in this implementation.

Table~\ref{tab:corridors} applies the current projection to five corridors at 18~knots in January and July.
The largest reduction against calm water is \CurrentBenefitMax\% (Hong Kong--Yokohama, January).

Jakarta--Melbourne is unchanged at the reported precision, because the schematic cores do not cover that track.
Seasonal factors move the deltas by a few tenths of a percent.
They do not create a second routing regime.

\subsection{Speed}
Figure~\ref{fig:pareto} is the Singapore--Tokyo lane (\LaneSingaporeTokyo~km) in January, clean hull, with current.
Fuel falls as speed is reduced, as expected from a resistance that increases faster than linearly with speed.
There is no interior fuel minimum on a fixed path: without a time cost, the tested minimum would lie at the lowest speed.
Equation~\eqref{eq:cost}, at \BunkerPrice~USD/t and \CharterRate~USD/day, has an interior minimum at \EconomicSpeed~knots.
That point uses \EconomicFuel~t of fuel and \EconomicHours~h, at a scenario cost of \EconomicCost~USD.
The same lane at 18~knots uses \ServiceFuel~t and \ServiceHours~h, at a scenario cost of \ServiceCost~USD.
Corresponding CO\(_2\) masses are \EconomicCOTwo~t and \ServiceCOTwo~t.
The difference is a consequence of the prices in \eqref{eq:cost}.
A higher charter rate, a schedule window, or an engine map that penalizes low load would move the minimum.
The figure does not identify a universally optimal speed.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The network explains the negative results.
Mean sea-vertex degree \MeanDegree{} means that, between two ports, the shortest lane path is usually close to a unique polyline.
Search can snap a voyage onto that polyline.
It cannot discover an alternative sea route that was never digitized.
The Jebel~Ali--Suez Canal path, which reaches 80\(^\circ\)E before turning into the Gulf of Aden, is the extreme case.
Reporting it as a routing benefit would be a misuse of the file.

The same geometry makes the combinatorial part small.
Once port-to-port distances are cached, seven hubs are an enumeration, not a reason to invoke a genetic algorithm.
The algorithm is documented because it is what the application runs, and because its crossover had to be replaced before that statement was true.
On larger optional-waypoint sets, or on a mesh with direction-dependent costs large enough to reorder ports, a heuristic would matter.
Neither condition holds here.
The current model, in particular, is too coarse, and too weak on these tracks, to change order.
A reanalysis product could.
This paper does not estimate how often it would.

Three modelling choices bound the fuel numbers.
First, specific consumption does not vary with load, so slow steaming looks slightly better than an engine map would allow.
Second, the fouling curve saturates by design.
Third, added resistance in waves is omitted from the reported fuel.
Including the application's sea-state bins would require a forecast and a baseline defined without a fixed percentage.
That comparison is not in the study.
The earlier 15\% figure was the fixed percentage itself.

A further bound is provenance.
The lane file cannot be dated or attributed from the repository.
Results that depend on it, including every distance in Table~\ref{tab:sequencing}, should be recomputed if the file is replaced.
The script that does so writes \texttt{paper/results.json}, \texttt{paper/numbers.tex}, and \texttt{paper/tables.tex}.
The manuscript inputs the latter two files, so a changed network cannot be silently described with the old sentences if the study is rerun and the manuscript recompiled.
Sentences that name a corridor, such as the Jebel~Ali--Suez Canal lengths, are checked against \texttt{results.json} rather than typed from memory; they are still ordinary text and should be re-read after a rerun.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

Shortest paths on the supplied lane graph are a well-posed distance problem.
A* and Dijkstra agree on all 380 port pairs.
Those distances exceed the great circle by a median factor of \MedianDetour, and by a factor of \MaxDetour{} on Jebel~Ali--Suez Canal, where the shortest stored lane detours to 80\(^\circ\)E before using the Red Sea.
Waypoint order is an open metric traveling-salesman problem.
On the instances tested it is solved exactly by enumeration and by nearest insertion.
A reversed order is longer by between \ReversedGapMin\% and \ReversedGapMax\%.
A schematic current changes fuel by a few percent and does not change order.
A Holtrop--Mennen fuel model without an ad-hoc scale factor gives about \FuelEighteen~t/day at 18~knots and \FuelTwenty~t/day at 20~knots for the stated Panamax particulars.
With the stated bunker and charter scenario, the Singapore--Tokyo cost minimum is at \EconomicSpeed~knots, not at the service speed used as a reference.
No percentage fuel saving from weather routing is claimed.
